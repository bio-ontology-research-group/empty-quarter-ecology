\section{Sample collection and processing}\label{sup:samplecollection}
\subsection{Field sampling}
At each site, we collected each field replicate in a sterile tube, either by scooping the sample directly into the tube or with a sterile plastic spoon. We collected samples in 2 mL tubes in T~1--T~4, and 15 mL tubes in T~5. During T~2--5, we taped the three replicate tubes together, so adjacent collections were separated by roughly one external tube diameter. Bulk subsurface soil was collected at a nominal depth of $5$--$10$~cm; the exact sampling depth of each sample was not recorded, and for the root-adjacent compartment, the exact distance from the root was not recorded, so the compartment separations are defined operationally by how each sample was taken rather than by fixed depths or distances. Samples were held at $-20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in an
Engel MR040F portable freezer during each expedition and transferred to
$-80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in the laboratory.

\subsection{Amplification and indexing}\label{sup:sampleprocessing}
The Bakt\_341F (\texttt{CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG}) and Bakt\_785R
(\texttt{GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC}) primers carried Illumina overhang adapters.
First-round PCR used TaKaRa Ex Taq DNA Polymerase (catalogue \texttt{RR001B})
in $50\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ reactions with $5\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of extracted DNA,
$5\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of $10\times$ Mg$^{2+}$-containing buffer, $4\,\mu\mathrm{L}$
of dNTP mix ($2.5\,\mathrm{mM}$ each), $2\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of each
$10\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ primer stock, $0.25\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of polymerase
($5\,\mathrm{U}/\mu\mathrm{L}$) and $31.75\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of nuclease-free
water, giving a final concentration of $0.4\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ per primer. The
programme was $95\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $3\,\mathrm{min}$; $35$ cycles of
$95\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $10\,\mathrm{s}$, $65\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for
$30\,\mathrm{s}$ and $72\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $1\,\mathrm{min}$; and
$75\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $5\,\mathrm{min}$. We checked products on a
$1\,\%$ agarose gel. Because most extracts had low or undetectable DNA, we set
template input by volume: samples without a visible amplicon were re-amplified
with $8\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ and then $12\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of DNA (with
$28.75\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ and $24.75\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ of water, respectively), and
samples without a product after the third attempt were classified as failed.

Indexing used the QIAGEN Multiplex PCR Kit (catalogue \texttt{206143}) with the following PCR conditions:
$94\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $15\,\mathrm{min}$; $8$ cycles of
$94\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $30\,\mathrm{s}$, $55\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for
$30\,\mathrm{s}$ and $72\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $30\,\mathrm{s}$; and
$72\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $5\,\mathrm{min}$.

\subsection{Library quantification, pooling and sequencing}
We purified libraries with Agencourt AMPure XP beads at a
bead-suspension-to-sample volume ratio of $0.8$:$1$, quantified them with the
Qubit high-sensitivity double-stranded DNA assay, and sized them on an Agilent
TapeStation~$4200$ with D5000 ScreenTape. We pooled libraries using the core
laboratory's molar-normalization spreadsheet, whose inputs were each library's
final DNA concentration and fragment size, the total pool volume and the target
molarity. For a pool of $N$ libraries, library $i$ with concentration $C_i$
contributed a volume $V_i = V_{\mathrm{pool}}C_{\mathrm{pool}}/(N C_i)$, where
$V_{\mathrm{pool}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{pool}}$ are the target pool volume and molar
concentration.

\subsection{Soil XRF}
\label{sup:XRF}
\subsubsection{Measurement workflows}
In-situ and laboratory XRF are distinct measurement workflows that differ in sample coverage, material, and analytical use, but both used the Vanta Handheld XRF Analyzer. The laboratory workflow measured stored soil samples from all campaigns on the bench, and provided $725$ records. The in-situ workflow measured soil in the field during T5 only, and provided $106$ measurements of which $71$ (at $58$ sites) passed quality control. These in-situ measurements record only the site of each reading, and do not correspond to any physical sample or compartment. Only laboratory XRF data are used for analyses, and the in-situ XRF is used as a descriptive T5 diagnostic.

The $725$ records from the laboratory XRF workflow were obtained as $547$ records from T1-T4 samples, and $178$ records from T5. All records were obtained in Fast mode, but T5 records additionally specify the acquisition settings (Fast Screening with a helium purge and $8$~mm beam, which aid detection of light elements). Where an element was measured more than once on a sample, T1–T4 records retained the largest positive reading and T5 retained the most recent reading. To assess sensitivity to the T5 reading-selection rule, we compared the last reported readings with readings marked as primary in the workbook. We found the results to be consistent for the $11$ affected elements (Spearman $\rho \geq 0.994$). The combined records do not contain concentration units, limits of detection, or the meaning of non-detect. Zeros entered the $\log(1+x)$ transformation as recorded, without a resolved detection limit interpretation.


\subsubsection{XRF element axis}
We retained the $11$ elements detected in at least $75\,\%$ of laboratory XRF records: Al, Ca, Cl, Fe, K, Mg, Na, P, S, Si, and Ti. We transformed their readings as $\log(1+x)$, and standardized each element within campaign. We then reduced the standardized elements to principal components and took the first component (PC1) as a single elemental score per sample (Table~\ref{tab:xrf_loadings}). PC1 captured $39.2\,\%$ of the elemental variance and was weighted most strongly toward Ca ($0.413$), Mg ($0.406$), Na ($0.370$), S ($0.354$) and Cl ($0.338$), and negatively toward Si ($-0.273$). This axis contrasts higher Ca, Mg, Na, S and Cl with lower Si. This score correlated $r = 0.825$ with an internal evaporite-element reference and $r = 0.976$ with a globally standardized version of the same axis.
We oriented PC1 to correlate positively with the mean within-campaign standardized log readings for Cl, Na and S, the retained elements in the internal reference set. The globally standardized sensitivity standardized each element across all campaigns together. Missing values and values constant within the standardization group were assigned zero after standardization.

\subsection{DNA extraction kit controls}\label{kitcontrols}
Since the study involved DNA extraction using different kits and extractions during different sessions, we included extraction blanks to understand contamination introduced during the extraction steps. We performed two sensitivity analyses using extraction blanks linked to samples from two different campaigns (T4 and T5), independently. We re-denoised the six T4 extraction blanks together with the additional control libraries and matched their ASVs to the biological count table by exact sequence. T5 samples and their extraction blanks shared a sequencing run. For both analyses, we considered an ASV as a candidate contaminant if it was present in at least $2$ blanks and was more prevalent in blanks than in samples using an unadjusted one-sided Fisher test ($p<0.1$). We quantified the candidate contaminant ASVs in the sample profiles, then removed them and measured the effect of their removal on Shannon diversity and raw richness using Spearman’s correlation coefficient to compare the sample profiles before and after their removal.

The T5 set included $17$ extraction blanks linked to $217$ samples, and we found $351$ candidate contaminant ASVs that comprised 2.2\% of pooled reads and a median of 0.40\% per profile (IQR 0.16—0.99\%, maximum 56.60\%). Before/after Shannon diversity and raw-richness rankings were strongly correlated ($\rho = 0.995$ and $0.99995$, respectively). T4 had $6$ extraction blanks linked to $95$ samples, and we found $7$ candidate contaminant ASVs, including \textit{Nesterenkonia}, \textit{Brevibacterium} and \textit{Alcaligenes} and $4$ ASVs without a genus assignment. Candidate contaminant ASVs were present in 33\% of the samples and comprised 0.14\% of pooled reads (maximum 14\%). Across the $95$ linked T4 profiles, before/after Shannon diversity and raw-richness rankings were strongly correlated (Spearman $\rho=0.99948$ and $1.00000$, respectively).


\subsection{Microbial community standard controls}
We included two different types of mock microbial community control samples in the study design. These controls included purified DNA ZymoBIOMICS HMW DNA Standard (D6322) in T1 and T2, and whole-cell ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (D6300) which we later extracted in T3. We used two community control replicates from T2 and two from T3 to estimate genus recovery based on the known community composition from the vendor. At the genus level, one T2 community control and two T3 controls recovered all the known genera present, with 31\%, 100\%, and  99.99\% of their reads assigned to the expected genera, respectively.

\subsection{Well-to-well contamination}
Use of 96-well plates for sequencing library preparation is known to introduce cross-contamination between physically close samples in microbiome studies \cite{welltowell}. We used $259$ biological samples from T1 and their well positions to evaluate whether well-to-well contamination occurred in our study. Within each campaign and 96-well plate, we compared Bray--Curtis dissimilarities between pairs of samples from different sites. We defined adjacent wells as pairs of wells sharing an edge or a corner (Euclidean distance $\le \sqrt{2}$), and we matched the pairs for geographic distance decile $\times$ compartment combination. We calculated mean difference in dissimilarity between adjacent and non-adjacent pairs, weighted by the number of adjacent pairs per stratum. Adjacent wells were not more similar than matched non-adjacent wells. Dissimilarity difference was +0.005 (999 permutations, one-sided $p$ = 0.87).

\section{Relic DNA experiment}\label{sup:relic}
\subsection{Sample description}
During T5, we collected additional samples from two campsites: C1R (root-adjacent soil) and C2R, C2S (root-adjacent soil, surface soil, respectively). We also collected C2 shallow sub-surface soil, but these samples were not included in the experiments because DNA extractions failed for these samples. For each of the three remaining parent soil tubes, representing a site $\times$ compartment group, we prepared $3$ separate homogenized soil slurries in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH $7.4$) at a $1:10$ (w/v) ratio. We split each slurry into a treated and an untreated aliquot, giving nine paired comparisons grouped within three parent tubes.

\subsection{PMAxx treatment}
We added PMAxx (Biotium, Hayward, USA) to each treated aliquot at a final concentration of $50\,\mu\mathrm{M}$. We subjected both treated and untreated aliquots to the following conditions: 10 min dark incubation, photo activation with a $450\,\mathrm{nm}$ LED lamp for 35 min on a white reflective surface ($15$--$20\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the lamp), and rotation at 15 min intervals. We stored samples at $-20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ until DNA extraction, which was performed using $860\,\mu\mathrm{L}$ from each aliquot with the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro kit (QIAGEN). We amplified the DNA and sequenced the samples with the protocols described in Section~\ref{sup:sampleprocessing}.

\subsection{Results}
We calculated Hurlbert expected richness at $123{,}897$ reads, the minimum
depth among the 18 libraries, and Shannon diversity at full read depth. We report each treated--untreated difference and the mean difference within each of the three site $\times$ compartment groups (Table~\ref{tab:relicresults}, Figure~\ref{fig:relicRichness}). The headline-estimate index is under \path{analysis/v3/headline_uncertainty/}.

\section{Microbial community diversity}
\subsection{Community Composition}\label{sup:composition}
We summed ASV counts at the phylum, class and genus levels using the SILVA
v138.2 assignments and calculated the mean relative abundance of each taxon
across the $1{,}227$ core profiles, overall, by compartment and by transect
third ($20$ sites each, ordered along the transect coordinate). Reads unassigned
at a given rank were retained as an unclassified category.

Eight phyla exceeded $2\,\%$ mean relative abundance
(Table~\ref{tab:taxa_phyla}). Bacillota and Actinomycetota together accounted
for $41.2\,\%$ of reads, whereas Acidobacteriota and Verrucomicrobiota, which
are among the most abundant phyla in soils globally, together accounted for
$3.3\,\%$. Cyanobacteriota contributed $0.4\,\%$ and were no more abundant in
surface soil ($0.40\,\%$) than in shallow-subsurface ($0.41\,\%$) or
root-adjacent soil ($0.51\,\%$). Archaea contributed $0.02\,\%$, as expected for
bacterial primers. \textit{Domibacillus} was the most abundant genus in every
compartment and in the western and central thirds, whereas \textit{Bacillus} was
the most abundant in the eastern third. Reads without a genus assignment made up
$35.6\,\%$ of reads. Full phylum-, class- and genus-level tables, overall and by
compartment and transect third, are given in Tables~\ref{tab:taxa_phyla}
and~\ref{tab:taxa_genera}.

\subsection{Alpha diversity (within-sample)}\label{sup:alphadiversity}
\subsubsection{Diversity indices and rarefaction}\label{sup:alphadiversity_indices}
We calculated Shannon diversity ($H$) with full read depths for all samples. We estimated rarefied richness as Hurlbert's expected number of ASVs at $25{,}000$ reads ($E[S_{25k}]$), and $55$ samples were removed as they fell below the selected read depth, resulting in $1{,}172$ samples with a richness estimate. We measured normalized Shannon as $H/\log(E[S_{25k}])$, where $H$ is Shannon diversity and $E[S_{25k}]$ is expected richness at $25{,}000$ reads. This mixed-depth ratio combines full-depth Shannon diversity with the logarithm of expected ASV richness at the common depth and can exceed $1$. Normalized Shannon ranged from $0.336$ to $0.945$, and it correlated only weakly with sequencing depth (Spearman's $\rho = 0.096$).

\subsubsection{Marginal and paired compartment contrasts}\label{sup:alphadiversity_compartments}
We estimated compartment differences using two approaches. First, we used a marginal estimate which averages within site and compartment across all campaigns, so the two compared compartments may come from different campaigns. Second, we used a paired estimate that restricts each contrast to the campaigns that included samples from both compartments at a site, averaged the difference within campaign, then averaged the campaigns within site (Table~\ref{tab:matched-alpha}). We report the paired estimate as the primary result because it removes campaign as a confound.

The two measures agree in direction throughout but differ in magnitude. The paired estimate shows lower estimated rarefied richness for root-adjacent soil in comparison to both bulk soil compartments, but in the marginal estimate the rarefied richness contrasts do not survive multiple-testing correction ($q = 0.81, 0.09, 0.07$ for root-surface, root-subsurface, and subsurface-surface). Normalized Shannon showed the most consistent signal under both estimates even after correction, where root-adjacent soil was lower than surface and shallow subsurface, and shallow subsurface was higher than surface soil. We obtained all intervals by site-level bootstrap and tested paired differences with the Wilcoxon signed-rank statistic, controlling the false discovery rate within a Shannon family and a normalized Shannon family separately.

\subsubsection{Potential confounds}\label{sup:alphadiversity_confounds}
To check whether read depth variation and DNA extraction kit differences contribute to compartment differences, we refitted the Shannon contrasts with Gaussian generalized estimating equations (GEEs) that included log read depth, campaign, and extraction kit, and used an exchangeable working correlation to account for repeated sampling of each site. The normalized Shannon contrasts, adjusted for campaign and log read depth, retained their direction and significance. A campaign $\times$ compartment interaction was significant for Shannon diversity before adjustment for read depth (Wald, $p = 0.008$) but not after ($p = 0.175$), indicating that part of the between-campaign variation in the Shannon contrasts may be attributed to read depth differences. The Shannon root-adjacent versus shallow subsurface contrast remained significant in every model we fitted for extraction kit, whereas the root-adjacent versus surface contrast in only three of the six (additive unadjusted, additive depth-adjusted, and extraction kit-matched pairs). We treat these adjustments as a sensitivity analysis rather than definitive controls, because extraction kit was recorded for $858$ of the samples and was partly confounded with campaign (one automated DNA extraction instrument was only used for samples from the last two campaigns), so sample processing, season, and campaign cannot be fully separated.

\subsubsection{Climate associations}\label{sup:alphadiversity_climate}
We first tested the correlation between our field weather measurements (air temperature, relative humidity, barometric pressure) and the three diversity indices. We averaged the measurements within site and campaign, and found that pressure correlated negatively with all diversity indices and humidity with Shannon diversity and richness, but none of the field-weather associations remained after correction when campaign and quadratic transect term were included with site-clustered standard errors. This is consistent with our assumption that these field measurements capture transient conditions rather than the climate under which microbial communities assemble.

We repeated this comparison using long-term climate data where we retrieved monthly measurements from Open-Meteo for mean air temperature, total rainfall, and mean relative humidity across the $60$ sites. (Section~\ref{main-sec:envmeasurements}). These covered $49$ consecutive months per site, from January 2022 to January 2026 (the last month’s data only covered 1-20 January 2026). We averaged the monthly measurements per variable into single long-term means for each site that describes its typical climate over a $\sim$~4 year period, which resulted in mean ranges of $26.38-30.30$$^{\circ}$C for temperature, $1.31-7.47$~mm per month for rain and $24.27-35.40\,\%$ for relative humidity. We also averaged the three alpha diversity measures (Shannon, rarefied richness, and normalized Shannon) first within site and campaign, then across campaigns. We then correlated each of the three climate means against each of the diversity means by Spearman rank across the $60$ sites, resulting in nine correlations corrected together as one Benjamini-Hochberg family (Table~\ref{tab:climate-alpha}). Every correlation is negative, and all effects remain after correction.

\subsubsection{Effect of controls on diversity}
Removing candidate contaminant ASVs from the linked T5 profiles (Section~\ref{kitcontrols}) preserved the rankings of Shannon diversity and raw ASV richness (Spearman correlation before and after of $0.995$ and $0.99995$, respectively); no profile fell below the rarefaction depth. In a paired PMAxx experiment (Section~\ref{sup:relic}) on three sample groups, $treated - untreated$ differences in rarefied richness and Shannon varied in direction and magnitude among groups and preparations. We report these as descriptive comparisons, since the design does not support a formal test.


\subsection{Short-term rainfall}\label{sup:rainfall}
\subsubsection{Rise-and-decay distributed lag kernel}\label{sup:rainfall_kernel}
To capture the delayed biological response of microbial communities to rainfall, we modeled alpha diversity measures (Shannon, rarefied richness, and normalized Shannon) as a function of time-dependent cumulative antecedent rainfall. We averaged the replicates for each of $617$ site $\times$ campaign $\times$ compartment combinations and ran the analysis independently for each of two rainfall datasets for comparison (Section~\ref{main-sec:envmeasurements}). For each group, we summed rainfall over the $60$ calendar days before the sample collection date under a rise-and-decay distributed lag kernel $(h(t))$ defined as:
\begin{equation*}
    h(t) = \left( \frac{t}{t_{\text{peak}}} \right) e^{1 - \frac{t}{t_{\text{peak}}}}
\end{equation*}
where $t$ represents the lag time in days, and $t_{\text{peak}}$ dictates the explicit target peak lag at which the weight reaches its maximum unit height $(1.0)$. To isolate the rainfall pulse from confounding background signals, we adjusted for site $\times$ compartment baselines, campaign means, and campaign-specific longitude and collection day trends $(0 + C({\text{Site}}):C({\text{Compartment}}) + C({\text{Campaign}}) + C({\text{Campaign}}):longitude\_z + C({\text{Campaign}}):sampling\_day\_z)$. To find the optimal response window without \textit{a priori} assumptions, we scanned $60$ candidate peak lags from $0.5$ to $30$ days in $0.5$ day increments for each of the three diversity indices, ranking candidates by the signed partial correlation, which orders models identically to the classical $t$ statistic because they share residual degrees of freedom. We calibrated the significance of this search against a complete calendar-year permutation distribution, described in Section~\ref{sup:rainfall_calendaryear}. We assessed sensitivity to site sampling by refitting the full model, including all nuisance coefficients, over $9{,}999$ whole-site bootstrap resamples, retaining all observations from each sampled site. We report central $95\%$ ranges across these refits; effect-size ranges retain the observed endpoint and peak, while separate refits assess endpoint and peak reselection.

While both datasets --- NASA POWER \texttt{PRECTOTCORR} and Open-Meteo --- provided daily rainfall, the NASA POWER dataset resolved the $60$ sites into $20$ distinct rainfall series due to its coarse grid, while Open-Meteo resolved them more finely into $58$ distinct series. Both datasets selected rarefied richness at an early lag (selected in $71.7\%$ and $68.0\%$ of resamples, against $25.8\%$ and $18.3\%$ for Shannon and $2.5\%$ and $13.7\%$ for normalized Shannon), agreeing on an early positive rarefied richness response but differing in magnitude and exact timing. When both endpoint and peak were reselected within a resample, the central peak ranges were $1.5$--$5.5$ days for NASA POWER and $0.5$--$12.5$ days for Open-Meteo. At the selected peak, each additional millimetre of peak-weighted rainfall was associated with $179.4$ additional expected taxa for NASA POWER and $326.8$ additional expected taxa for Open-Meteo (Table~\ref{tab:rainfall-uncertainty}).

\subsubsection{Complete calendar-year permutation calibration}
\label{sup:rainfall_calendaryear}
Because we selected the strongest lag, endpoint and product from a large search array, a nominal significance test could inflate the Type I error rate. To calibrate the entire search rather than the selected model, we constructed a null that breaks the correspondence between rainfall timing and community composition while preserving the seasonal and spatial structure of the rainfall. We enumerated all six permutations of the three complete years available across every collection date in both products ($2023$, $2024$ and $2025$), where each meteorological year spans $1$ December of the preceding year through $30$ November. Mapping a collection date to another year preserves its month and day, so that its exposure draws on the preceding $60$ days of the reassigned year. We applied the same year assignment across all sites, compartments and both products, keeping expeditions that share a year coupled, which preserves the seasonal position of sampling and the shared calendar rainfall field. The February campaign requires the preceding December and therefore excludes $2022$, and the final series excludes the incomplete $2026$ year. The six permutations include the observed assignment, and we computed exact one-sided probabilities as the fraction of assignments with a search statistic at least as large as observed, ties included, and this quantity has a minimum attainable value of $1/6$.

Under this null, the observed rainfall association was not distinguishable from the permuted assignments. Table~\ref{tab:rainfall-calendar} contains the exact probabilities, and the absolute maximum searches returned the same three values. With only three complete years available, the finite permutation orbit contains six arrangements, so $1/6$ ($\approx 0.167$) is the smallest probability this design can produce, and the test cannot reach conventional significance thresholds. We therefore interpret the calendar-year permutation as a coarse-resolution test under the assumption that the annual rainfall fields are exchangeable.

To confirm that the exact-ranking procedure was correctly calibrated under its own null, we applied it to $300$ simulated community datasets containing site, campaign and spatially correlated campaign errors, assigning each dataset an independent, uniformly drawn year permutation. At nominal thresholds of $0.05$, $0.20$ and $0.50$, the procedure rejected $0$, $55$ and $152$ of the $300$ datasets, respectively (exact binomial $95\%$ intervals $0$--$1.22\%$, $14.12$--$23.18\%$ and $44.86$--$56.46\%$), matching the rejection probabilities of $0$, $1/6$ and $1/2$ permitted by the finite orbit. This confirms the procedure controls the error rate under the specified randomized-year null, and the field analysis additionally assumes exchangeability of the three observed annual rainfall fields.

\subsubsection{Robustness checks}
To assess the sensitivity of the early-lag rarefied richness association, we ran three additional checks. First, to test whether the fitted peak was an artifact of the kernel shape, we replaced the rise-and-decay weighting with rainfall summed over seven fixed antecedent windows and repeated the endpoint search. Second, to test whether the association reflected a genuine antecedent effect rather than a spurious correlation, we constructed a negative control in which rainfall was drawn from the period \textit{after} collection, which cannot plausibly influence the sampled community. Third, to assess sensitivity to low-biomass contaminants, we removed candidate contaminant taxa (Section~\ref{kitcontrols}) and refitted the model.

The fixed-window model placed the largest positive estimate at $3$--$4$ days with NASA POWER and at $1$ day with Open-Meteo. The post-collection negative control was weak for NASA POWER, whereas Open-Meteo gave a richness association of comparable size (maximum $t=4.38$, partial $R^2=0.043$, versus $0.038$ for antecedent rainfall). This product therefore supports an association around the collection date with uncertain timing. Removing candidate contaminant taxa left the antecedent estimate essentially unchanged. Allowing quadratic or cubic longitude trends reduced the NASA POWER estimate to $71$--$124$ taxa per mm; the site-clustered interval for the quadratic-longitude model included zero ($-0.15$ to $141.4$ taxa per mm). As an independent check on the rainfall exposure itself, we compared five rainfall datasets (CHIRPS v2.0, CMORPH V1.0, GPM IMERG Final V07B, NASA POWER and Open-Meteo) over the four complete days preceding Trip~$1$ collection. Every pair was positively correlated across the $60$ sites (Pearson $r = 0.501$--$0.917$; Spearman $\rho = 0.554$--$0.881$), indicating that the datasets describe a consistent spatial exposure.

\subsubsection{Wet spell attribution}
To identify which ``wet spells'', periods of consistent rainfall, contributed to the fitted association, we removed each regional wet spell in turn and repeated the complete search. We defined a wet spell as a maximal sequence of consecutive calendar days with recorded rainfall at any site wihtin one campaign's $60$-day pre-collection window, and removed its rainfall jointly across all sites and compartments. This produced $29$ deletions for NASA POWER and $17$ for Open-Meteo.

Across these deletions, the largest positive partial correlation over the $180$ models ranged from $0.0443$ to $0.2030$ for NASA POWER and from $0.1471$ to $0.1963$ for Open-Meteo. Removing the wet spell of $10$--$19$ March $2023$ produced the smallest maximum in each product, identifying it as the principal influence on the fitted early-lag association. These descriptive deletions locate the influential rainfall periods but cannot separate the biological responses to individual events; independent sampling would be required to estimate variation among responses to separate rainfall events.

\subsection{Beta diversity (between-sample)}\label{sup:beta}

\subsubsection{Data preparation, resampling and multiple testing}\label{sup:beta_methods}
We summed sequencing replicates within each site $\times$ campaign $\times$
compartment combination and excluded profiles with fewer than $2{,}000$ reads,
leaving $630$ profiles from the $60$ core sites. Campaigns contributed $25.2$,
$2.5$, $26.8$, $27.9$ and $17.5\,\%$ of these profiles. For the compositional
analyses, we ranked genera present in at least $20\,\%$ of profiles by mean
relative abundance ($384$ eligible genera) and retained the top $200$ as the
primary set. We CLR-transformed the raw counts of the retained genera after
adding a pseudocount of $0.5$, so each genus is expressed relative to the
geometric mean of the retained genera. The turnover--nestedness partition and
core occupancy did not use this transformation
(Section~\ref{sup:beta_turnover}).

We used the following resampling settings: $999$ whole-site permutations for
the transect RDA; $9{,}999$ joint site permutations for distance decay and the
turnover--nestedness regressions; $999$ within-site permutations for the
compartment PERMANOVA and the pH and elemental-axis partial RDAs; and $999$
sign flips for all paired compartment tests. We obtained intervals for the RDA
variance explained and for distance-decay slopes from delete-one-site jackknife
standard errors ($95\,\%$ $t$ intervals), removing every profile or distance
involving the omitted site. Intervals for compartment displacements and
dispersion differences are percentile intervals from $2{,}000$ site bootstrap
resamples. We applied Benjamini--Hochberg correction within separately defined
families: the three pairwise compartment location contrasts, the three
dispersion contrasts, the $600$ genus-level compartment contrasts, and each set
of $200$ genus correlations (transect, pH and elemental axis). Random seeds are
recorded in the repository.

\subsubsection{Transect model}\label{sup:beta_transect}
We converted site latitude and longitude to kilometres, centred them, and took
the score on their first principal axis as the transect coordinate, with its
sign set so that it increases eastward. The primary RDA explained $40.1\,\%$ of
the variation among site profiles (delete-one-site jackknife $95\,\%$ interval
$32.4$--$47.7\,\%$; $p=0.001$). We repeated the RDA with the $80$, $200$ and all
$384$ eligible genera, with linear and with quadratic trends, and after
excluding each campaign in turn; all fits gave $p=0.001$. At ASV resolution, the same model on the same $630$ profiles and $60$ sites explained $37.3\,\%$ of the variation with the $800$ most abundant ASVs ($95\,\%$ interval $32.1$--$42.5\,\%$) and $35.4\,\%$ with $2{,}000$ ASVs ($30.4$--$40.3\,\%$), compared with $40.1\,\%$ at genus level; all fits gave $p=0.001$. Of the $200$ genera, $124$ changed monotonically along the
transect ($q<0.05$; Spearman correlation of site-level CLR values with the
transect coordinate): $77$ decreased and $47$ increased eastward. The decreasing
genera were mainly Actinomycetota ($27$) and Pseudomonadota ($20$); the
increasing genera were mainly Bacillota ($17$) and Pseudomonadota ($16$)
(Table~\ref{tab:taxa_replacement}).


\subsubsection{Residual spatial autocorrelation}\label{sup:beta_spatialerrors}
The RDA residuals were spatially autocorrelated (multivariate Moran's
$I=0.065$, $p=0.001$, five-nearest-neighbour graph). With $3$--$6$ neighbours, $I=0.050$--$0.110$ ($p\leq0.002$); with $8$ and $10$ neighbours, $p=0.050$ and $0.243$, respectively. Site permutations assume that sites are
independent, so we re-evaluated the transect trend under spatially correlated
Gaussian error models \cite{langsrud2005rotation}. Each model combined a
site-level spatial covariance with independent profile-level noise. We varied
the noise fraction ($0.01$, $0.10$, $0.50$, $0.90$), the kernel (exponential,
Mat\'ern $3/2$, Mat\'ern $5/2$) and its range (the $14.4$-km median
nearest-site distance and $5$, $10$, $20$, $50$ and $100\,\%$ of the
$1{,}014$-km transect extent). Together with a noise-only model this gave $73$
models. Each model incorporated the centring and site averaging applied to the
data, and we tested the trend by rotation ($9{,}999$ rotations). The trend was supported ($p=0.0001$) at the $14.4$-km range and whenever $90\,\%$ of variance was independent noise. With $50\,\%$ noise, $p$ ranged from $0.0019$ to $0.183$ at ranges of $203$~km or more; with $\leq10\,\%$ noise and ranges of $50$~km or more, $p$ ranged from $0.0045$ to $1.000$ (Table~\ref{tab:spatial_covariance}). Under those models, a smooth
spatial field cannot be distinguished from the transect trend, so we report the
variance explained by the transect as descriptive.

To check the error rates of these procedures, we simulated $500$ null datasets
per generating model on the observed coordinates and sampling design ($199$

rejected $3.8$--$5.6\,\%$ of null datasets. Unrestricted site permutation

\subsubsection{Distance decay}\label{sup:beta_decay}
We restricted the analysis to sites with sample profiles in all three compartments ($1{,}770$ site pairs). Aitchison dissimilarity increased with geographic distance in every compartment (each $p=0.0001$), at $1.349$ per $100$~km in surface soil ($95\,\%$ CI $0.903$--$1.795$), $1.507$ in shallow-subsurface soil ($1.089$--$1.925$) and $1.175$ in root-adjacent soil ($0.695$--$1.656$). The omnibus test of slope differences gave $p=0.0051$. After maximum-statistic correction, shallow-subsurface decay exceeded root-adjacent decay by $0.332$ per $100$~km ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.0039$). The difference between surface and root-adjacent soil ($+0.174$) was not supported ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.173$).


\subsubsection{Compartment differences}\label{sup:beta_compartment}

\textbf{PERMANOVA and pairwise location tests.} The PERMANOVA used the $170$ campaign $\times$ site blocks with all three compartments across $60$ sites (pseudo-$F=17.28$, $p=0.001$). Each pairwise location test used every block containing both compartments of the pair: $183$ blocks for shallow-subsurface versus surface, $182$ for root-adjacent versus surface, and $191$ for root-adjacent versus shallow-subsurface.

For each pair, we summarised how consistently compartments differed across sites as a standardized displacement:
\begin{equation*}
    \lVert\overline{d}\rVert /
\overline{\lVert d_i\rVert}
\end{equation*}
where $d_i$ is the site-level difference vector. This is the length of the mean difference vector divided by the mean length of the site-level difference vectors. It ranges from $0$, when site-level shifts point in opposing directions and cancel, to $1$, when every site shifts in the same direction. Standardized displacement was $0.331$ (shallow-subsurface versus surface), $0.433$ (root-adjacent versus surface) and $0.478$ (root-adjacent versus shallow-subsurface), each with $p=0.001$ and $q=0.001$ (site bootstrap intervals in Table~\ref{tab:compartment-displacement}).

We repeated the tests with $80$, $200$ and all $384$ eligible genera, with pseudocounts of $0.5$ and $1.0$ and with multiplicative zero replacement, and separately after excluding each campaign at the primary settings. For each refit, we compared the direction of the mean difference vector with the primary analysis. All contrasts remained supported, and their directions were unchanged.

\textbf{Dispersion.} Dispersion increased from surface to shallow-subsurface to root-adjacent soil. Paired differences were $1.378$ for shallow-subsurface minus surface ($95\,\%$ CI $0.441$--$2.375$; $q=0.015$), $2.387$ for root-adjacent minus surface ($1.550$--$3.212$; $q=0.003$) and $1.009$ for root-adjacent minus shallow-subsurface ($0.056$--$2.055$; $q=0.061$).

\subsection{Turnover, nestedness and core occupancy}\label{sup:beta_turnover}
We pooled raw counts of all genera within site and compartment across campaigns and rarefied each pooled community once to the smallest pooled library ($12{,}865$ reads). Mean S\o{}rensen dissimilarity was $0.401$ (surface), $0.406$ (shallow-subsurface) and $0.395$ (root-adjacent). The turnover component was $0.286$, $0.280$ and $0.295$, accounting for $69$--$75\,\%$ of S\o{}rensen dissimilarity. Both components increased with distance. Nestedness therefore increased faster with distance in the two bulk compartments, and turnover faster in root-adjacent soil.

\subsection{Composition and environment}\label{sup:beta_environment}

\subsubsection{Soil pH}\label{sup:beta_ph}
$709$ pH measurements were linked to $560$ campaign $\times$ site $\times$ compartment groups across all $60$ sites (mean pH $7.99$). Transect position explained $84.3\,\%$ of pH variation after accounting for campaign and compartment ($p=0.001$). In the partial RDA, pH explained $0.278\,\%$ of the remaining compositional variation ($p=0.125$). We partitioned variation in the adjusted site-average composition using a common response denominator: $1.38\,\%$ was uniquely associated with pH, $13.56\,\%$ uniquely with geography, $23.86\,\%$ shared and $61.20\,\%$ unexplained. Across all $560$ groups, with equal total weight per site and including within-site variation, the corresponding fractions were $0.31\,\%$, $6.78\,\%$, $4.76\,\%$ and $88.15\,\%$.

For these partitions, we summed genus counts within campaign $\times$ site $\times$ compartment groups, retained groups with at least $2{,}000$ reads, and selected the $200$ most abundant genera present in at least $20\,\%$ of groups. We transformed counts to CLR values with pseudocount $0.5$. Each site's groups had total weight one. Four weighted models fitted campaign-by-compartment categories alone, categories with pH, categories with linear and quadratic transect terms, and all predictors. Differences in residual sums of squares yielded the unique and shared fractions, divided by the residual sum of squares of the category-only model. For the site-average partition, we first adjusted the CLR response and each predictor against the same category design and averaged their residuals within each site; the denominator was the total variation of this fixed site-average response. These are raw descriptive fractions for two distinct responses.

We assessed sampling sensitivity with $1{,}999$ whole-site bootstrap draws and $1{,}999$ draws for each of three non-circular contiguous-block schemes of $5$, $10$ or $15$ sites ordered along the transect. Each draw retained all groups from a selected site and re-estimated the category adjustment, keeping feature selection and transformation fixed. The resampling ranges describe sensitivity to the sampled sites. Removing one site at a time left the unique pH fraction at $1.25$--$1.63\,\%$ for site averages. The shared component ranged from $15.13$ to $32.14\,\%$ across the central $95\,\%$ of whole-site bootstrap draws and from $0.22$ to $31.38\,\%$ with $15$-site blocks. The longer blocks span a median $253$~km and retain only four drawn blocks, so the partition is sensitive to the sampled portion of the transect. Exact inputs, methods and all resampling results are provided under \path{analysis/v3/ph_partition_20261004/}.

Of the $200$ genera, $117$ correlated with site-mean pH ($q<0.05$), $104$ of which also correlated with transect position (Table~\ref{tab:ph_genera}).

\subsubsection{Landform}\label{sup:landform}
Each site has one assigned landform: $44$ sand dunes, $9$ saline pans (three western, one central, five eastern), $3$ eastern oases, and one each of gravel plain, dune slack, aeolian lake and oil-spill site (Table~\ref{tab:landform_alpha}).

\textbf{Alpha diversity.} Dune and saline pan sites did not differ in Shannon diversity, rarefied richness or normalized Shannon diversity according to Mann--Whitney tests, which gave $p=0.35$, $0.28$ and $0.93$, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:landform_alpha}). The three oasis sites had the lowest diversity (mean Shannon $4.25$, compared with $5.77$ at dune sites), which we report descriptively as there are only three such sites.

\textbf{Composition.} Saline pans differed from sand dunes before adjustment for transect position (pseudo-$F=4.56$, $p=0.0013$), with $16$ genera differing at $q<0.05$. Examples of genera more abundant in sand dunes were \textit{Herpetosiphon}, \textit{Oxalophagus}, \textit{Opitutus} and \textit{Symbiobacterium}, examples of genera more abundant in saline pans were \textit{Thermobaculum}, \textit{Truepera} and \textit{Enterococcus}. After adjustment, the difference was smaller (pseudo-$F=1.78$, $p=0.032$) and no genus passed $q<0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:landform_sensitivity}).

\textbf{Dune-only refits.} On the $44$ dune sites, the transect RDA explained $34.2\,\%$ of compositional variation (compared with $40.1\,\%$ across all sites), and $70$ of the $71$ genera with supported transect correlations changed in the same direction as in the full dataset. Of the nine climate--diversity correlations, the six involving temperature and humidity and the rainfall--rarefied richness correlation remained supported ($q<0.05$); rainfall--Shannon ($q=0.141$) and rainfall--normalized Shannon ($q=0.459$) did not (Table~\ref{tab:landform_climate}).

\section{Functional potential}\label{sup:function}

\subsection{Prediction input and quality}\label{sup:function_prediction}
We generated PICRUSt2 v2.4.1 predictions from two input tables (Trips~$1$--$4$ and Trip~$5$ ampliseq outputs; the Trip~$5$ input contained $330{,}830$ ASVs), and all $1{,}227$ samples were matched to a prediction. The weighted NSTI had a median of $0.165$ across sample profiles (range $0.005$--$0.782$), with medians of $0.201$ in shallow-subsurface, $0.168$ in surface and $0.134$ in root-adjacent profiles. The execution parameters, including hidden-state method, placement settings and NSTI cut-off, are archived with the analysis.


\subsection{Comparison with metagenomes}\label{sup:function_validation}
The companion shotgun study sequenced $150$ Trip~$5$ libraries ($27$ surface, $44$ shallow-subsurface and $79$ root-adjacent) and recovered a catalogue of $991$ metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). We estimated genome abundances in each shotgun library with CoverM and annotated the catalogue using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12. On average, $28.5\,\%$ of shotgun reads mapped to the catalogue, so the MAG-derived community profiles summarize the recovered fraction of each library.

For the whole-profile comparison, we used $990$ genomes with KO annotations to construct an abundance-weighted community KO profile for each shotgun library. We compared the $125$ libraries with matched amplicon profiles across $3{,}471$ shared KOs. The marker-gene comparison used the intersection of $975$ genomes with the required annotation and abundance data and $119$ shotgun libraries with matched amplicon profiles. We correlated predicted and MAG-derived community abundances for each of the $20$ marker-gene sets, with confidence intervals from $9{,}999$ bootstrap resamples of sites that kept libraries from the same site together.



\subsection{Pathway composition}\label{sup:function_pathways}
Of the $462$ predicted pathways, $416$ were present in at least $20\,\%$ of the $633$ grouped sample profiles. We repeated the transect and compartment analyses, first with the $100$ most abundant pathways, then with all $416$ prevalent pathways. We added a pseudocount of $1.0$ and excluded each campaign in turn. Unlike the community composition analysis, the pairwise compartment tests for pathways were computed within the $172$ complete three compartment blocks, each centred on its three compartment mean, rather than within pair-specific blocks. Intervals for standardized displacement came from $2{,}000$ site-level bootstrap resamples.

We assigned pathways to classes by matching keywords in their MetaCyc descriptions: biosynthesis (\textit{biosynthesis}), degradation or utilization (\textit{degradation}, \textit{utilization}, \textit{assimilation}), energy and central metabolism (\textit{respiration}, \textit{fermentation}, \textit{glycolysis}, \textit{TCA cycle}, and \textit{carbon-fixation cycles}), and other. These predicted pathway abundances are not independent as pathways can share gene families.

\subsection{Marker-gene sets}\label{sup:function_traits}
\path{analysis/v3/trait_genes_20260909/trait_definitions.tsv} lists the KOs in each of the $20$ marker-gene sets. The hydrogenase set combines \textit{hyaB}, \textit{hhyL} and \textit{hoxH}, and therefore includes both membrane-bound group~1 and soluble group~3d [NiFe]-hydrogenases. Because a genome counts as a carrier when it contains any KO in a set, multi-gene sets (for example, osmolyte synthesis) indicate partial rather than complete pathways. We calculated community carrier abundance as the summed relative abundance of carrier genomes among the genomes recruiting reads in each library. Genome-level results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:traits_genomes} and PICRUSt2 results in Table~\ref{tab:traits_picrust}.
